# Why cities should bury their power lines

Look up on almost any city street and you will see the same thing: a row of wooden poles, a tangle of wires, and a transformer hanging like a grey bucket from a crossarm. We stop noticing them the way we stop noticing a hum in the walls. But the poles are a choice, and cities made that choice more than a century ago because hanging wire was the cheapest way to carry power. The cities of that day ran on lamps and telephones. Ours run on pumps, screens, and heating, and the choice has outlived its reasons.

This essay argues that cities should bury their power lines, starting with the oldest neighbourhoods, where the wires are most tangled and the poles most worn. Buried lines fail less often in storms, hurt fewer people, and leave streets that people want to live on. The one serious objection is cost, and I will take it last, because it is the part of the argument that looks strongest and holds up worst.

The 1998 ice storm shows what is at stake. Freezing rain coated every wire and branch in the region, and the weight of the ice brought down 1,200 kilometres of line. In all, 90,000 homes were without power for 28 days. Families moved in with relatives, shops threw out their stock, and the pumps at the water plant stopped. The ice was the trigger, but every failure began with a wire hanging in open air.

## Reliability

Overhead lines are exposed to everything the weather and the road can throw at them. Wind snaps branches onto the wires, ice loads the spans until they sag or break, and a single car can take down a pole and the power to a whole block. When a branch lands on teh line, every customer downstream goes dark until a crew arrives, and crews cannot start work until the road is clear. Underground cable sits below the frost line, out of reach of all of this.

A fair worry is that buried cable is slower to repair, and that is true. A crew can see a broken pole from the street, but a fault in a buried line has to be located with instruments and then dug up. What the worry leaves out is how rarely a buried line fails. Cable in conduit has no wind, ice, or branches to fail from, so a buried network has far fewer outages to repair, and most of the repairs it does need can wait for daylight and dry weather.

Reliability matters more every year because more of daily life now runs on power. Traffic signals, water pumps, phone towers, and hospital refrigeration all stop when a line comes down, and a storm that once cost a household a cold evening can now cost a clinic its medicine. Cities spend heavily to keep hospitals and water plants running on generators, but generators need fuel, and fuel runs out. It is fairer to protect the lines that feed the whole neighbourhood, so that it stays lit, not only the buildings with a backup.

### Safety

The danger of overhead lines is easy to forget because it is spread so thin. A pole struck by a truck, a branch that pulls a wire down, or a transformer that fails in the heat can leave a live conductor on the ground, and a live wire on the ground looks like any other cable. Utilities tell people to keep well back and call for help, which is good advice for an adult and no help at all for a child or a dog.

The people who answer those calls know the problem best. A crew arrives at a burning house and cannot raise a ladder until the utility confirms the line is dead, a spark from a failing pole sets dry brush alight on a windy night, a car that hits a pole leaves the driver trapped under a live wire, a lineworker climbs a pole in the dark to repair a fault, and every one of these dangers goes away when the cable is buried.

Buried lines bring a different risk, which is digging, and I do not want to wave it away. A contractor who strikes a cable with a backhoe can be badly hurt, and the strike can cut power to a district. The answer is a record of where every cable runs and a rule that nobody digs without checking it, and most cities already require this for gas and water mains. That is a smaller and more manageable danger than a live wire loose in a street during a storm.

## Aesthetics and property values

Wires are ugly, and the ugliness is not only a matter of taste. Poles and wires cut across every view of a street, from a front window to a church tower. A pole carries a transformer, a phone line, and a cable line, and the bundle sags a little more each year. Wires also force the utility to cut trees back from the lines, so a maple trimmed around a wire grows lopsided, and it's crown never recovers. Where the lines are buried, the same street can hold a full canopy, and the shade it casts cools the pavement in summer.

A clear view has a price, and buyers pay it. Houses on streets without poles tend to sell faster and for more than houses on streets with wires, and shopping streets gain in the same way. Shopfronts and signs are no longer hidden behind a web of cable, and a town centre that looks cared for draws more visitors. Owners recieve the benefit at once, in the price they ask, though nothing about the house itself has changed. The city gains later, as a larger tax base on the same land.

Poles also take up space, and a street with space to spare looks better and works better. A pole in the middle of a narrow footpath forces a wheelchair or a pram into the road, and the steel cables that brace a corner pole are a trap for anyone not looking up. Buried cable frees that space. The strip once taken by a pole can hold a bench, a bike rack, a bus shelter, or another tree, and the lamp posts that remain can be chosen for their looks, not for whether they can carry a wire.

## Costs

Cost is the strongest objection, and I will give it its full weight. A kilometre of underground line costs 1.8 million dollars, against 320,000 dollars for a kilometre of overhead line. That is more than five times as much, and it is the figure that ends the discussion in most council chambers. No city can bury every line at once, and none has to. A price is only a reason to say no when it is set beside what the buyer gets, and the sections above have described what the buyer gets.

The 1.8 million dollars splits into three parts, and the split shows where the money goes. First, trenching and conduit cost 1.1 million dollars per kilometre. Second, cable and switching equipment cost 0.5 million dollars per kilometre. Third, restoring the road and the footpath afterwards costs 0.2 million dollars per kilometre.

A price per kilometre also hides how long the line lasts. Underground cable is built to last 40 years, and a cost that serves for 40 years should be read as 40 smaller costs. Spread over that time, the 1.8 million dollars comes to 45,000 dollars per kilometre per year. The 320,000 dollars for an overhead line pays only for building it. The trimming, the inspection, and the storm repairs follow every year of its life, and none of them is in that figure.
