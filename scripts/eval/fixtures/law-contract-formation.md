# Contract Formation at Common Law: A Primer

A contract is a promise the law will enforce, and the first question in any contract dispute is whether one was formed at all. At common law the answer turns on four elements: an offer, an acceptance of that offer, consideration, and an intention to create legal relations. This primer takes them in order, then works through acceptances sent by post, and ends with the reasons behind the rules. The cases are English; American law reaches the same results on most points.

## Offer

An offer is a statement of the terms on which a person is willing to be bound, made so that a simple yes will close the deal. It must be distinguished from an invitation to treat, which only invites offers. Goods on a shop shelf are an invitation to treat: in Pharmaceutical Society v Boots (1953), the customer made the offer at the till. An advertisement can be an offer when its terms are definite, as in Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co (1893). An offer can be revoked at any time before it is accepted, but the revocation takes effect only when the offeree learns of it.

## Acceptance and the mirror image rule

An acceptance must agree to the offer exactly as made. This is the mirror image rule: a reply that says yes but changes the price, the quantity, or the delivery date is not an acceptance. An acceptance must also, as a rule, be communicated to the offeror, and silence is not acceptance: in Felthouse v Bindley (1862), an uncle who offered to buy his nephew's horse and said he would take silence as a yes had no contract when the nephew never replied.

A reply that fails the mirror image rule is a counteroffer, and a counteroffer does two things. It is a new offer, and it rejects the original offer, which then ceases to exist. In Hyde v Wrench (1840), Wrench offered to sell his farm for £1,000. Hyde replied offering £950, and Wrench declined. Hyde then wrote that he accepted the original £1,000. The court held there was no contract: Hyde's counteroffer had killed the £1,000 offer. A mere request for information, such as whether delivery could be spread over two months, is not a counteroffer and leaves the offer open.

## Consideration

Each side must give something in exchange for the other's promise: a promise, an act, or a forbearance. The courts ask only whether the exchange exists, not whether it is fair. In Chappell & Co v Nestlé (1960), three chocolate wrappers were part of the price of a record. Past consideration does not count: a promise to pay for work already finished, with no request beforehand, is a gift.

## Intention to create legal relations

Commercial agreements are presumed to be binding; family and social arrangements are presumed not to be. In Balfour v Balfour (1919), a husband's promise of a monthly allowance to his wife was unenforceable. Either presumption can be rebutted.

## The mailbox rule

For an acceptance sent by post, the need for communication gives way to the postal rule from Adams v Lindsell (1818): the acceptance takes effect the moment it is posted, not when it arrives. A revocation of an offer, by contrast, takes effect only when it is received, as Byrne v Van Tienhoven (1880) held. The rule applies only where post is a reasonable way to accept, and the offeror can exclude it by requiring actual receipt.

On 1 March, Alder Ltd posts an offer to sell 500 tonnes of timber to Birch & Co at £200 a tonne. The offer arrives on 3 March. On 4 March, Birch posts its acceptance. On 5 March, Alder changes its mind and posts a revocation, which reaches Birch on 6 March. Birch's acceptance reaches Alder on 7 March. A contract was formed on 4 March, when Birch posted the acceptance. Alder's revocation is too late: it could take effect only on 6 March, when it was received, two days after the contract existed.

## Why the law draws these lines

Formation rules exist to answer one question with confidence: at what moment may each party rely on the deal? The law judges agreement objectively, by what the parties said and did, not by what they privately meant, because a party can only act on what it can observe. Where a rule must put a loss on one of two innocent parties, it tends to put it on the one who could have avoided it more cheaply. The offeror who chooses to deal by post can protect itself by demanding actual receipt; the offeree who has posted an acceptance cannot know whether a revocation is on its way. The rules are defaults, and careful parties write around them.
