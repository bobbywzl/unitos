[0:51–1:23] @Lecturer So, today's topic is congestion pricing. The idea is simple. A city picks a zone, usually the busy center, and charges every vehicle that enters it during busy hours. The charge is a price on road space, and like any price, its job is to ration something scarce.
[1:23–1:57] @Lecturer Notice what I did not say. The city does not charge to raise money, and it does not charge to punish drivers. The main purpose is to change how many people drive into the zone at the busiest times. Money is a side effect.
[1:57–2:29] @Lecturer Let me give the one sentence version. Congestion pricing makes a driver pay for the delay that driver causes other people. Right now that delay is free for the driver and expensive for everyone else on the road. Pricing moves the bill to the person who caused it.
[2:29–2:59] @Lecturer Now the argument, and it rests on one idea, the difference between private cost and social cost. When you decide to drive downtown at eight in the morning, you count your own costs. Fuel, wear on the car, parking, and your own time stuck in traffic.
[2:59–3:30] @Lecturer What you do not count is the cost you add to everyone else. Your car takes up road space, so every other driver, and every bus, moves a little slower. Each of them pays for that with there own time, and none of them is paid back.
[3:30–4:00] @Lecturer Economists call that an externality, a cost that lands on people outside the decision. So the true cost of your trip, the marginal social cost, is your private cost plus the external cost you impose on everyone else. The driver sees only the first part.
[4:00–4:26] @Lecturer Why marginal? Because the decision is about one more car, not the average car. On a jammed road, the margin cost of one more car is far above the average cost, because that one car slows all the others.
[4:26–4:59] @Lecturer Let me make this concrete, and I will keep these same numbers for the rest of the lecture. Take a downtown zone, and I will use the numbers from Marlow, a city we will meet again later. On a typical weekday, 400,000 cars enter the zone during the busiest hours.
[4:59–5:29] @Lecturer For the driver, the marginal cost of one trip is 20 dollars, counting fuel, parking, and time. But one extra car at that hour adds about 15 dollars of delay and exhaust for everyone else. So the marginal social cost of one trip is 35 dollars.
[5:29–5:47] @Lecturer Um, let me find the slide. It was right here a minute ago. Hold on. Okay, I think it is in the other folder. Give me a second.
[5:47–6:15] @Lecturer Here is the point. If the driver faces only 20 dollars, every trip worth more than 20 dollars to the driver gets made, even a trip worth 25 dollars. That trip costs society 35 dollars. So society loses 10 dollars every time that trip happens.
[6:15–6:37] @Lecturer The fix is a charge equal to the external cost. Set the charge at 15 dollars. Now the driver faces 20 plus 15, which is 35, the same as the true social cost.
[6:37–7:05] @Lecturer Now what happens to traffic? At 35 dollars all in, the drivers whose trips are worth less than 35 stop coming. In Marlow that was 20 percent of the cars. So entries into the zone fall from 400,000 to 320,000 each weekday.
[7:05–7:35] @Lecturer Fewer cars means the buses are no longer stuck in the same queue. In Marlow, bus speeds went up 12 percent. That is a real gain, and it is a gain for people who never paid the charge, because a faster bus is a better bus.
[7:35–8:04] @Lecturer Now the money. 320,000 cars a day, about 250 charging days a year, at 15 dollars each. That is 80 million paid trips and revenue of 1.2 billion dollars a year. Please write that one down.
[8:04–8:35] @Lecturer Marlow's law earmarks 80 percent of that revenue for transit. Earmarked means the law fixes the use in advance, so the money cannot drift into the general budget. That is 960 million dollars a year for buses and trains, and the other 240 million dollars covers running costs.
[8:35–9:09] @Lecturer So who gains? Drivers who stay pay 15 dollars and get a faster trip. Bus riders get a faster bus. Drivers who drop out lose a trip worth less than 35 dollars. Add it up, and society is better off, because every trip we removed cost more than it was worth.
[9:09–9:39] @Lecturer Notice this is different from an ordinary toll on a bridge. A toll is usually flat, and its job is to pay for the bridge. A congestion charge changes with the traffic. It is highest when the road is most jammed, and nothing at night.
[9:39–10:11] @Lecturer You might ask why only 20 percent of the cars disappear. Most drivers who stay have real reasons to be downtown, and few good alternatives on their route at eight in the morning. Economists call that inelastic demand. It responds to price, but not by much.
[10:11–10:41] @Lecturer How does a city find the right number? It estimates the external cost, sets a first charge, then watches the traffic and adjusts. Marlow landed on 15 dollars after that process. It is not a magic number.
[10:41–10:56] @Lecturer Someone always says, why not just build more roads? Hold that thought, because the end of the lecture answers it.
[10:56–11:18] @Lecturer Let me show you three cases. I picked them because they went three different ways. Marlow worked well, Kestrava ran into a boundary problem, and Halvoren failed politically. Same economics, three different outcomes.
[11:18–11:51] @Lecturer Marlow first. The city built the transit upgrades before it turned the charge on. New bus lanes, more trains at rush hour, and a park and ride lot at the edge of the zone. So drivers had something to switch to on day one.
[11:51–12:12] @Lecturer How does Marlow collect the charge? Cameras read license plates at the edge of the zone, and drivers pay online by midnight. There are no toll booths, so nothing slows the traffic.
[12:12–12:40] @Lecturer After the first year, Marlow reported what the model predicted. The charge was 15 dollars. Cars entering the zone fell 20 percent, buses ran 12 percent faster, and the scheme raised 1.2 billion dollars a year, with 80 percent earmarked for transit.
[12:40–13:10] @Lecturer The politics mattered as much as the economics. Before the launch, most Marlow residents opposed the charge. A year later, most supported it, once they saw the faster buses and where the money went. People trust a price more when they see what it buys.
[13:10–13:37] @Lecturer Second case, Kestrava, an old walled town. Kestrava drew its zone tightly around the old walls, because that was the part everyone complained about. The charge worked inside the walls. Traffic dropped, the streets were quieter, and the air was cleaner.
[13:37–14:09] @Lecturer The trouble started at the edge. Drivers who used to drive into the old town now parked just outside the walls, and the streets around the boundary filled up with cars. The old ring road, which had no charge, carried many of the cars that used to go through the center.
[14:09–14:42] @Lecturer Kestrava fixed it in two steps. It moved the boundary out to the ring road, so the zone covered the real congestion, and it added a cheap shuttle from the edge lots into the old town.
[14:42–15:10] @Lecturer Third case, Halvoren. Halvoren copied the equipment from Marlow but not the design. It charged a flat tole at every hour of the day, so the price did not change when the roads were jammed, and the money went into the general budget.
[15:10–15:36] @Lecturer Peak traffic hardly moved. A flat toll is too low at eight in the morning and too high at midnight, so it fixes neither problem. Drivers saw a new cost and no benefit, because bus service stayed the same.
[15:36–16:06] @Lecturer Two years later, Halvoren held a vote and repealed the scheme. Honestly, I do not blame the voters. The city had charged people and given them nothing. The difference from Marlow was the design and the earmark.
[16:06–16:31] @Lecturer Yes, in the second row. The question is whether parking fees already do this job, since they also make drivers pay for coming downtown. It is a fair question, so I will take a short detour to answer it.
[16:31–17:00] @Lecturer A parking fee only reaches cars that stop in the zone. A car passing through, a van that never parks, or a driver with a private garage pays nothing, yet each of them takes up road space and slows the bus just as much.
[17:00–17:32] @Lecturer So the better comparison is a toll booth, not a parking meter. A toll booth charges everyone who passes, whether they stop or not. A congestion charge does the same thing, only it uses cameras and changes with the time of day. Okay, back to the main line.
[17:32–17:58] @Lecturer Now the objections. There are two serious ones, and you will hear them in every city that tries this. The first is that the charge is regressive. The second is that it only moves the problem to the boundary.
[17:58–18:30] @Lecturer Regressive means the charge takes a bigger share from poor households than from rich ones. The charge is the same 15 dollars for everyone, but 15 dollars is a small bite of a high income and a big bite of a low one. That is a real concern.
[18:30–18:59] @Lecturer I have three answers. First, look at who actually drives into the zone at peak hours. In most cities it skews richer than the average resident, because lower income commuters are already on the bus. So the charge falls mostly on higher income drivers.
[18:59–19:31] @Lecturer Second, design can soften it. Marlow gives a discount to low income drivers and to people who depend on a car for work, such as home care nurses and tradespeople with tools. The discount keeps most of the traffic effect, because it covers a small share of trips.
[19:31–19:59] @Lecturer Third, follow the money. In Marlow, 80 percent of the revenue is earmarked for transit, so the people who ride buses every day, who are mostly lower income, get a faster bus and more service.
[19:59–20:21] @Lecturer So my answer to the first objection is not that it is wrong. It is that design and the earmark can answer it, and a free road is not fair either.
[20:21–20:49] @Lecturer The second objection is boundary effects. Draw a line on a map, and people will adjust to the line. Some will park just outside it, some will drive around it, and the roads next to the boundary will take the extra cars.
[20:49–21:09] @Lecturer That is what happened in Kestrava. Answer one is to draw the zone around the real congestion, and to put the edge where a detour costs more than the charge.
[21:09–21:24] @Lecturer Sorry, um, hold on. I lost the slide again. Let me scroll back, uh, no, not that one. Okay, give me a second.
[21:24–21:50] @Lecturer Answer two is to make the edge a place to switch, not to dodge. Put park and ride lots and fast transit just outside the zone, so a driver who stops there can still reach downtown without paying the charge.
[21:50–22:18] @Lecturer Answer three is to measure the roads around the boundary and adjust. If traffic rises on a ring road, widen the zone or put a smaller charge on that road. A boundary is a policy you tune, not a law of nature.
[22:18–22:42] @Lecturer That brings me to the last idea for today, and it explains why the charge has to stay on. The idea is induced demand. It is usually explained with highways, so let me define it there first.
[22:42–23:08] @Lecturer Induced demand is the extra driving that appears when driving gets cheaper or faster. When a city adds road capacity, trips become quicker, so people make trips they would have skipped, and the new traffic fills the new space.
[23:08–23:34] @Lecturer That is the answer to why not just build more roads. Congestion falls at first, then climbs back, because the lower cost of a trip brings new trips. The gain is spent on more driving, not faster driving.
[23:34–24:00] @Lecturer Now apply that to Marlow. The 20 percent of cars that left freed up road space. Drop the charge, and induced demand would fill that space again, and the buses would slow down. The charge keeps the space free.
[24:00–24:27] @Lecturer Let me pull the argument together. A driver counts the private cost, 20 dollars. The marginal social cost is 35 dollars, because each extra car adds 15 dollars of delay for everyone else. A charge of 15 dollars closes the gap.
[24:27–24:51] @Lecturer In Marlow that charge cut the cars entering the zone by 20 percent and sped up the buses by 12 percent. It raised 1.2 billion dollars a year, and 80 percent of that is earmarked for transit.
[24:51–25:17] @Lecturer Three lessons from the cases. Build the alternatives first, as Marlow did. Draw the zone around the real congestion, as Kestrava learned. And earmark the money, because Halvoren shows what happens when residents see a cost and no benefit.
[25:17–25:46] @Lecturer A congestion charge is not mainly a tax on driving. It is a price for a scarce resource that has been given away free, and the sign of success is a road that moves, not the money raised.
