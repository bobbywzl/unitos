# Why Bond Prices Fall When Interest Rates Rise

Savers who buy bonds for safety are often surprised to see their bond fund lose money in a year when nothing defaulted. The cause is almost always a rise in interest rates. This article shows why a rise in rates lowers the price of a bond you already own, how to tell in advance how much it will fall, and what the yield curve says about rates in the years ahead.

## A bond is a fixed stream of payments

A bond is a loan you make to a government or a company, and its payments are fixed the day it is issued. Take a 10-year bond with a face value of $1,000 and a 3 percent coupon. It pays $30 a year for ten years and then returns the $1,000. When it is issued, other 10-year bonds also yield 3 percent, so it sells for its face value, $1,000.

## When rates rise

Now suppose market rates rise to 5 percent right after you buy it. New 10-year bonds pay $50 a year on $1,000. Nobody will pay $1,000 for your bond's $30 a year when $50 is available at the same price, so to sell it you must accept less. The price falls until a buyer earns 5 percent on it: about $845. At that price the buyer collects the same $30 coupons plus a gain of $155 at maturity, and together they work out to 5 percent a year. The price of a bond is the sum of its future payments, each discounted at the current market rate, so a higher rate makes every payment worth less today. The same logic runs in reverse: if rates fell to 1 percent, the bond would be worth about $1,189. The longer a bond has to run, the more payments are marked down and the larger the fall.

| 10-year | 8.5 | $845.57 | −15.4% |
| 30-year | 19.6 | $692.55 | −30.7% |

## Duration: how much the price moves

Duration measures a bond's sensitivity to rates. Its meaning is practical: a bond with a duration of 8.5 changes in price by about 8.5 percent for each one-point change in interest rates, falling when rates rise and rising when they fall. The 10-year bond above has a duration of about 8.5, so a one-point rise costs about 8.5 percent, and the two-point rise from 3 to 5 percent should cost about 17 percent, taking the price to about $830. The actual price, about $845, is a little higher. The gap is convexity: the curve of price against rate bends, so duration overstates the loss when rates rise and understates the gain when they fall. Duration is longer for bonds with longer maturities and lower coupons, and every bond fund publishes its average duration. A fund with a duration of 6 will lose about 6 percent if rates rise by one point.

## The yield curve

The yield curve plots the yields of bonds of the same issuer, usually the U.S. Treasury, against their maturities, from three months to 30 years. Its normal shape slopes upward: a 2-year note yields less than a 10-year note, which yields less than a 30-year bond. Lenders ask for extra yield to lock their money away longer, partly because, as the duration section shows, longer bonds lose more when rates rise. Sometimes the curve inverts, and short maturities yield more than long ones. In July 2023 the 2-year Treasury yielded about 4.9 percent and the 10-year about 3.9 percent. An inverted curve means investors expect rates to fall, often because they expect the economy to slow, and an inversion has come before most U.S. recessions since the 1970s.

## Our view

We think the lesson of the last few years is simple and was learned the hard way. In 2020 the 10-year Treasury yielded less than 1 percent, and many savers reached for long bond funds to squeeze out a little more income. Then rates rose faster than at any time in four decades, and the broad U.S. bond market lost about 13 percent in 2022, its worst year on record. Nobody who read the duration on the fund's fact sheet should have been surprised. Our advice is to match the duration of your bonds to the date you will need the money. If you need it in two years, a 20-year bond fund is a bet on rates, not a safe place to wait.
