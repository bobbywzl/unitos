# The Action Potential

A neuron sends a signal down its axon as an action potential: a brief reversal of the voltage across its membrane that travels from the cell body to the axon terminals. Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley worked out its mechanism in 1952 from recordings in the giant axon of the squid. This section starts from the membrane at rest, follows one action potential through its phases, shows how it travels, and ends with the pump that pays for it.

## The resting potential

At rest the inside of the axon sits at about −70 mV relative to the outside. Two ion gradients set this voltage. Potassium ions are about 28 times more concentrated inside than outside, and sodium ions about 10 times more concentrated outside than inside. At rest the membrane is far more permeable to K+ than to Na+, because K+ leak channels stay open. K+ ions leak out and leave a surplus of negative charge behind, which pulls the inside toward the K+ equilibrium potential of about −90 mV. A small leak of Na+ inward holds the resting potential a little above that value, at −70 mV.

| Ion | Inside (mM) | Outside (mM) | Equilibrium potential |
|---|---|---|---|
| K+ | 140 | 5 | about −90 mV |
| Na+ | 15 | 145 | about +60 mV |

## The sequence of an action potential

An action potential begins when a stimulus depolarizes the membrane to the threshold of about −55 mV. At threshold, voltage-gated Na+ channels open. Na+ rushes in down its gradient, which depolarizes the membrane further, which opens more Na+ channels: a positive feedback loop that drives the voltage up to about +30 mV in less than a millisecond. The voltage never reaches the Na+ equilibrium potential of +60 mV, because about a millisecond after opening, each Na+ channel closes its inactivation gate and stops passing current. By then the slower voltage-gated K+ channels have opened. K+ flows out, and the membrane repolarizes back toward rest. The K+ channels close slowly, so for a few milliseconds the membrane overshoots to about −80 mV, closer to the K+ equilibrium potential. This undershoot is the hyperpolarization. Then the membrane returns to −70 mV. The spike itself lasts about 2 ms.

The refractory period follows from the same channels. While the Na+ channels are inactivated, no stimulus of any strength can start a second action potential; this absolute refractory period lasts about 1 ms. During the undershoot, the Na+ channels have recovered but the membrane is further from threshold, so only a stronger stimulus can fire the neuron; this is the relative refractory period. The refractory period limits how often a neuron can fire and, as the next section shows, keeps an action potential moving forward.

## Propagation along the axon

In an unmyelinated axon, the action potential spreads patch by patch. Na+ entering at one patch flows along the inside of the axon and depolarizes the neighbouring patch to threshold, which then fires. The patch behind the action potential is refractory, so the signal can only move forward. Every patch must fire in turn, so conduction is slow: about 1 m/s in a thin unmyelinated axon.

A myelinated axon is wrapped in myelin, layers of membrane made by Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system and by oligodendrocytes in the brain and spinal cord. Myelin insulates the axon and blocks ion flow, except at gaps about 1 µm wide called nodes of Ranvier, spaced about 1 mm apart and packed with voltage-gated Na+ channels. Current from one node flows quickly under the myelin to the next node and brings it to threshold, so the action potential fires only at the nodes and seems to jump from node to node. This is saltatory conduction, from the Latin saltare, to leap. It reaches up to about 100 m/s in large myelinated axons, a hundred times faster than the unmyelinated axon above, and it saves energy, because ions cross the membrane only at the nodes.

## Restoring the gradients

Each action potential lets a little Na+ in and a little K+ out. The amounts are tiny compared with the ions in the cell, so a neuron can fire thousands of times before its gradients run down. The sodium–potassium pump rebuilds the gradients from the first section. For each molecule of ATP it uses, it moves 3 Na+ ions out of the cell and 2 K+ ions in, both against their gradients. The pump runs all the time, not once per spike, and because it moves more positive charge out than in, it makes the inside a few millivolts more negative. The brain uses about 20 percent of the body's energy at rest, and a large share of that goes to these pumps.
