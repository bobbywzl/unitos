# Bretton Woods: The Rise and Fall of the Dollar–Gold System

For about a quarter of a century after the Second World War, the world's major currencies were tied to the U.S. dollar and the dollar was tied to gold. The arrangement was designed in July 1944 at a conference in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, and it ended in two steps, in 1971 and 1973. This article describes how the system worked, the flaw built into it, and how it came apart.

## The conference

The United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference opened on 1 July 1944 at the Mount Washington Hotel, a resort in the White Mountains hurriedly readied for the meeting. Some 730 delegates from 44 Allied nations filled it for three weeks, while the war in Europe was still being fought. Henry Morgenthau, the U.S. Treasury Secretary, presided, but the conference belonged to two men. Harry Dexter White of the U.S. Treasury chaired the commission that designed the fund; John Maynard Keynes, leading the British delegation, chaired the commission on the bank. Keynes had arrived with his own plan for a world clearing union and a new reserve currency, the bancor, and saw most of it set aside in favour of White's. He was 61 and in poor health; his wife, the ballerina Lydia Lopokova, came with him. The agreements were signed on 22 July. Keynes died less than two years later, in April 1946, and White in August 1948.

## The system agreed in 1944

The system had two layers. At the bottom, each member country set a par value for its currency against the U.S. dollar and promised to keep its market exchange rate within 1 percent of it, buying or selling dollars as needed. At the top, the United States promised to convert dollars held by foreign central banks into gold at a fixed $35 per troy ounce. Every currency was thus linked to gold through the dollar. A country could change its par value only to correct a fundamental disequilibrium in its balance of payments, and only after consulting the fund.

The conference also created two institutions, both of which came into being in December 1945. The International Monetary Fund pooled gold and currencies from its members and lent them to a country with a temporary deficit, so that it could defend its peg without deep spending cuts or controls on trade. The International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, now part of the World Bank, lent to rebuild Europe and later for development.

## The Triffin dilemma

The Belgian-American economist Robert Triffin pointed out a flaw in 1959 and 1960. As world trade grew, central banks needed more reserves, and under the system those reserves were mostly dollars. The only way for the rest of the world to acquire more dollars was for the United States to run balance-of-payments deficits, spending and investing abroad more than it earned. But every dollar added to foreign reserves was a claim on the U.S. gold stock, which was not growing. Once foreign dollar holdings exceeded the gold behind them, the promise to pay $35 an ounce could not be kept for everyone. If the United States stopped its deficits, the world would run short of reserves; if it continued them, confidence in the dollar would erode.

The dilemma played out as Triffin predicted. By 1960 dollars held abroad exceeded the U.S. gold stock, and in October of that year the price of gold in London briefly rose to about $40 an ounce. The United States and seven European central banks formed the London Gold Pool in 1961 to hold the market price at $35, but it collapsed in March 1968. The U.S. gold stock, about $25 billion in 1949, fell to about $10 billion by 1971.

## The Nixon shock

On Sunday, 15 August 1971, President Richard Nixon announced on television that the United States would stop converting dollars into gold for foreign central banks. This closing of the gold window removed the top layer of the system. The same speech imposed a 10 percent surcharge on imports and a 90-day freeze on wages and prices.

## To floating rates

The Smithsonian Agreement of December 1971 tried to rebuild the system without gold convertibility: the dollar was devalued to $38 an ounce, and currencies could move 2.25 percent either side of their new rates. It did not hold. In February 1973 the dollar was devalued again, to $42.22 an ounce, and by March 1973 the major currencies were floating against one another, their rates set by the market. The Jamaica Accords of 1976 agreed to change the IMF's rules to allow floating. The end of gold convertibility resolved the Triffin dilemma by dropping one of its horns, yet the dollar remained the world's main reserve currency.
