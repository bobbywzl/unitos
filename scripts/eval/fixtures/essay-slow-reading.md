# The case for reading one thing slowly

Most advice about reading is about reading more. Read faster, read daily, read two books a month. I want to argue the opposite: the reading that changes what you can do is the reading of one thing slowly, and almost nobody does it.

Here is what I mean by slowly. You take one document that matters to your work, a paper, a contract, a report, and you read it until you could rewrite it. Every claim, you can state in your own words. Every number, you know where it came from and what it rests on. Every step in the argument, you can say why it follows, or why it does not. That takes hours for a twenty-page paper. It took me eleven hours for the first paper I read this way, and I had read it twice before at the ordinary speed.

The ordinary speed gives you the shape of the argument and the feeling of having understood it. It does not give you the argument. I know this because when I tried to use that paper, I got the method wrong in a way the paper itself warned against, in a sentence I had read twice. Reading at speed, the warning registered as a caveat. Reading slowly, it turned out to be the point.

Slow reading has a cost, and the cost is everything else you did not read. Eleven hours is three books at the ordinary speed. The trade is worth it only for documents you will act on. A paper you will build on, a contract you will sign, a report you will decide from: those are the ones. For everything else, the ordinary speed is right, and the feeling of understanding is all you need.

Two habits make slow reading possible. The first is writing while reading: not highlights, which mark what seemed important, but notes that restate what the text says, in your words, with the source beside them. If you cannot restate a passage, you have not understood it, and the note tells you so. The second is asking one question of the whole document at a time, and reading for that question only. A question makes a twenty-page paper into six passages, and six passages can be read slowly in an hour.

The objection I hear most is that this does not scale. It does not. That is the point. The documents that matter to your work are few, and they deserve the hours. The rest deserve the ordinary speed, and no guilt.
