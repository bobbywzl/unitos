# Who Pays a Tariff on Steel?

A tariff is a tax on imports, and the law says the importer pays it at the border. That settles who writes the cheque, not who bears the cost. The cost is shared between domestic buyers, who pay a higher price, and foreign sellers, who receive a lower one. This article works out the split for a 25 percent tariff on imported steel, the rate the United States set in 2018, and measures what the tariff destroys along the way.

## The market for imported steel

The buyers are domestic carmakers, appliance makers, and builders. Their demand curve slopes down: the higher the price, the less imported steel they buy, because they can use less steel, switch to aluminium, or buy from domestic mills. The sellers are foreign steel mills. Their supply curve slopes up: the higher the price, the more steel they ship here rather than to other markets. Before the tariff, the market clears where the two curves cross, at $800 per ton and 1,000,000 tons a year.

## Who bears the tax

A tariff drives a wedge between the two curves. Buyers now pay one price and sellers receive a lower one, and the gap between them is the tariff. How the wedge splits depends on the elasticities of the two curves, that is, on how strongly each side responds to price. The side that can adjust less bears more of the tax. If buyers have no good substitutes, they keep buying at a higher price and bear most of it. If foreign mills can easily sell their steel in other countries, they refuse to cut their price by much, and again the buyers bear most of it. As a rule, the buyers' share of the tax is Es/(Es + |Ed|), where Es is the elasticity of supply and Ed the elasticity of demand.

## A worked example

Suppose the demand for imported steel has an elasticity of −0.4 and the foreign supply an elasticity of 1.5, so supply responds 3.75 times as strongly as demand. With the 25 percent tariff, foreign sellers receive $760 per ton, and buyers pay $760 plus 25 percent of $760, or $950. The tariff is $190 per ton. Of that, buyers bear $150, the rise from $800 to $950, and foreign sellers bear $40, the fall from $800 to $760. The buyers' share is 150/190, or 79 percent, exactly the 1.5/(1.5 + 0.4) that the rule above predicts. Imports fall by 7.5 percent, from 1,000,000 to 925,000 tons a year, and the government collects $190 × 925,000, or about $176 million a year.

| Measure | Before the tariff | After the tariff |
|---|---|---|
| Price buyers pay | $800 | $950 |
| Price foreign sellers receive | $800 | $760 |
| Imports | 1,000,000 tons | 925,000 tons |
| Tariff revenue | $0 | $175.75 million |

## Deadweight loss

The buyers lose more than they pay in tax, and so do the sellers. Buyers lose $150 on each of the 925,000 tons they still buy, plus the surplus they used to get on the 75,000 tons they no longer buy: about $144 million of consumer surplus in all. Foreign sellers lose about $38.5 million of producer surplus. The government gains $175.75 million. The losses exceed the gain by $7.1 million a year. That difference is the deadweight loss, the area of the triangle between the demand and supply curves from 925,000 to 1,000,000 tons: one half times the $190 wedge times the 75,000 tons, or $7,125,000. It measures trades that were worth more to buyers than they cost foreign mills to supply, and that no longer happen. Nobody collects it. Note also that $37 million of the revenue, the $40 per ton the foreign sellers bear, comes out of foreign pockets. That is more than the whole deadweight loss, which is why a large importer can come out ahead from a tariff if its trading partners do not retaliate.

## A different view

Not everyone reads these numbers as a case against the tariff. As one trade columnist put it to us, the triangles are the wrong scorecard. A tariff, in this view, is good politics: its costs are spread thinly over millions of buyers who never see a line for it on a receipt, while its benefits land on a few steel towns that notice every job. A politician who protects those towns wins their votes and loses almost none elsewhere. The columnist sees nothing cynical in that, calling it democracy responding to the people who care most, and argues that economists who count only surplus miss what voters are asking for.
