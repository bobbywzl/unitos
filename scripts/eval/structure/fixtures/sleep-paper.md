# A 90-Minute Nap Outperforms Caffeine for Afternoon Memory Consolidation: A Randomized Controlled Trial in University Students
L. Hartmann, J. Okafor, M. Chen, and R. Silva, Department of Psychology
Keywords: nap, caffeine, memory consolidation, slow-wave sleep, university students
Abstract. Students often use caffeine to get through the afternoon, yet sleep, not alertness, is thought to drive the consolidation of new memories. We randomly assigned 120 university students to a 90-minute nap, 200 mg of caffeine, or a placebo after they learned a word-pair list and a motor sequence at 13:00. Recall was tested at 17:00 and again the next morning. The nap group retained more word pairs than the caffeine group (d = 0.71, p < .001) and the placebo group (d = 0.84, p < .001), while caffeine did not differ from placebo on word-pair retention (d = 0.12, p = .58). Caffeine improved reaction time on the 17:00 vigilance task but did not improve motor-sequence retention. Retention gains in the nap group correlated with time spent in slow-wave sleep (r = .46). The advantage of the nap held at the next-morning test. A full-cycle nap is a better afternoon strategy than caffeine when the goal is to keep what was learned.
## Introduction
Memory consolidation is the process by which newly encoded information becomes stable and resistant to interference. A large body of work shows that sleep after learning supports consolidation, especially for declarative memories such as word pairs and facts.
Our first claim is that the benefit of sleep for declarative memory depends on slow-wave sleep. During slow-wave sleep, hippocampal replay is thought to transfer new memories to neocortical stores, and the amount of slow-wave sleep after learning has predicted next-day recall in several overnight studies.
Our second claim is that caffeine, although it raises alertness, does not support consolidation and may impair it. Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors, reduces sleep pressure, and in overnight studies reduces slow-wave sleep. Students who drink coffee after a lecture may feel sharper but may keep less of what they learned.
Most nap studies have used short naps of 10 to 30 minutes, which rarely reach slow-wave sleep, and few have compared a nap directly with caffeine in the same design. We tested a 90-minute nap, long enough for one full sleep cycle, against a typical dose of caffeine and a placebo, with both a same-day and a next-day test.
## Methods
### Participants
We recruited 120 undergraduate students (78 women, 42 men; mean age 20.4 years, SD = 1.6) through a university participant pool. Inclusion criteria were habitual sleep of 6 to 9 hours, caffeine intake under 300 mg per day, no sleep disorder, and no psychiatric or neurological diagnosis. Participants were paid and gave written informed consent. The study was approved by the university ethics board.
Of 163 students screened, 31 did not meet the sleep or caffeine criteria and 12 declined after the information session. No participant was excluded after randomization.
### Design
The study used a randomized, between-subjects design with three groups of 40: nap, caffeine, and placebo. Caffeine and placebo were given double-blind in identical capsules. Participants slept at home the night before with actigraphy to confirm normal sleep, abstained from caffeine for 24 hours, and arrived at the laboratory at 12:30.
A researcher not involved in testing generated the allocation sequence in blocks of six, stratified by sex. Testers did not know whether a waking participant had taken caffeine or placebo.
At 13:00 participants learned 60 word pairs to a criterion of 60% correct and practiced a five-element finger-tapping sequence. At 13:30 the nap group slept in a dark room with polysomnography for up to 90 minutes. The caffeine and placebo groups took their capsule at 13:30 and watched neutral nature documentaries for the same period.
All participants stayed in the laboratory until the 17:00 test, ate a standard snack at 15:15, and were asked not to nap, drink caffeine, or study the word pairs before the day-2 test.
### Measures
Word-pair retention was the percentage of pairs recalled at test relative to the last learning trial. Motor-sequence retention was the change in correct sequences per 30 seconds from the end of training to test. Alertness was measured with a 10-minute psychomotor vigilance task (PVT) and the Karolinska Sleepiness Scale. Tests took place at 17:00 on day 1 and at 09:00 on day 2.
The word pairs were semantically related nouns (for example, "ocean - salt") drawn from a normed pool and matched across two list versions, which were counterbalanced across groups.
In the nap group, polysomnography recorded total sleep time and the minutes spent in N1, N2, slow-wave sleep (N3), and REM sleep.
Two trained scorers, blind to retention scores, staged the recordings in 30-second epochs using standard criteria; agreement was 91% (kappa = .86).
### Analysis
We used one-way ANOVAs with planned contrasts (nap vs. caffeine, nap vs. placebo, caffeine vs. placebo) for each outcome, with Holm correction across contrasts. Effect sizes are Cohen's d. Within the nap group, we correlated retention with minutes of each sleep stage. The sample of 40 per group gave 80% power to detect d = 0.63 at alpha = .05. All analyses were preregistered.
As a secondary analysis, we repeated the contrasts with baseline learning score as a covariate; the results did not change in direction or significance.
For the day-2 test, we analyzed the 117 participants who attended; the three missing participants did not differ from the rest on day-1 retention.
## Results
All 120 participants completed the day-1 session; 117 completed the day-2 session (one nap, one caffeine, and one placebo participant missed it). The groups did not differ at baseline in learning performance, F(2, 117) = 0.41, p = .66, or in self-reported sleep the night before, F(2, 117) = 0.88, p = .42.
In the nap group, mean total sleep time was 78.6 minutes (SD = 9.2). Participants spent 21.3 minutes (SD = 8.1) in slow-wave sleep and 12.4 minutes (SD = 7.7) in REM sleep. Thirty-four of the 40 nappers (85%) reached slow-wave sleep, and 29 (73%) reached REM sleep.
At 17:00, word-pair retention was 91.2% (SD = 6.4) in the nap group, 85.9% (SD = 8.2) in the caffeine group, and 84.6% (SD = 9.1) in the placebo group. The omnibus effect was significant, F(2, 117) = 8.27, p < .001. The nap group retained more than the caffeine group (d = 0.71, p < .001) and the placebo group (d = 0.84, p < .001). Caffeine did not differ from placebo (d = 0.12, p = .58).
Motor-sequence retention showed a smaller pattern. The nap group improved by 11.8% from training to test, the caffeine group by 6.1%, and the placebo group by 5.4%. Nap differed from placebo (d = 0.52, p = .02) but not significantly from caffeine after correction (d = 0.43, p = .07).
Caffeine improved alertness. At 17:00 the caffeine group had faster mean PVT reaction times (284 ms) than the placebo group (311 ms; d = 0.66, p = .004) and the nap group (302 ms; d = 0.45, p = .048). Nappers reported more grogginess in the first 15 minutes after waking, but Karolinska Sleepiness Scale scores did not differ between groups by 17:00, F(2, 117) = 1.12, p = .33.
Within the nap group, word-pair retention correlated with minutes of slow-wave sleep (r = .46, p = .003) but not with REM sleep (r = .09, p = .58) or total sleep time (r = .21, p = .19). At the day-2 test, the nap group still retained more word pairs than the caffeine group (82.7% vs. 76.3%; d = 0.58, p = .01), and the caffeine group still did not differ from placebo (76.3% vs. 75.1%; d = 0.10, p = .66).
## Discussion
A 90-minute nap after learning led to better retention of word pairs than 200 mg of caffeine or a placebo, and the advantage held the next morning. Caffeine made students faster on a vigilance task but did not help them keep what they had learned. Alertness and consolidation came apart.
This dissociation explains why caffeine feels helpful. Students judge their learning by how alert they feel, and caffeine changes that feeling without changing what the brain keeps.
The findings support our first claim. Retention in the nap group tracked minutes of slow-wave sleep, not total sleep or REM sleep, which fits the view that slow-wave sleep carries the consolidation of declarative memory. The six nappers who never reached slow-wave sleep had retention close to the placebo mean, though this subgroup is too small to test.
The findings partly support our second claim. Caffeine did not support consolidation, but it did not impair it either: retention in the caffeine group was slightly, not significantly, higher than placebo. Because the caffeine group stayed awake, our design cannot test whether caffeine reduces the benefit of sleep itself; that would need a nap-plus-caffeine group.
One result contradicts prior work. Earlier studies reported that REM sleep in a nap supports motor-sequence learning and that a nap matches caffeine on motor tasks. We found a smaller nap benefit for motor sequences, no correlation with REM sleep, and no significant difference from caffeine. One explanation is that our 90-minute nap gave less REM sleep than the longer naps in those studies; another is that our finger-tapping task was too easy to show a ceiling-free REM effect.
The practical message is direct. A student who needs to stay sharp for a 15:00 meeting may reach for coffee. A student who wants to remember the 13:00 lecture tomorrow is better served by a full sleep cycle, accepting a short period of grogginess after waking.
Future work should add a nap-plus-caffeine group, test longer material such as lecture content, and run the design across a semester to see whether regular naps change exam performance.
## Limitations
- The sample was university students with regular sleep, so the results may not generalize to older adults, shift workers, or people with sleep disorders.
- The caffeine and placebo groups watched documentaries while the nap group slept; quiet wakefulness with eyes closed would be a closer control.
- We tested a single caffeine dose (200 mg) and did not measure blood caffeine levels.
- The study did not include a nap-plus-caffeine group, so it cannot say whether caffeine reduces the benefit of a nap.
- Only one declarative task and one motor task were used; results may differ for more complex material such as text comprehension.
- Sleep inertia after the nap may have masked a larger benefit at an earlier test; we did not test before 17:00.
- Participants knew whether they had napped, so expectations about naps may have affected effort at test.
- The six nappers who did not reach slow-wave sleep were too few for a separate analysis.
## Conclusion
In 120 university students, a 90-minute nap after learning improved afternoon and next-morning retention of word pairs more than caffeine or a placebo, and the benefit scaled with slow-wave sleep. Caffeine raised alertness but did not improve memory consolidation. When the goal is to keep what was learned, a full-cycle nap is the better afternoon choice.
For universities, the results argue for quiet nap spaces near lecture halls as much as for coffee stands.
