Phil 235N: Nietzsche Notes
Note: Untimely Meditations was written almost 10 years before The Gay Science.
Week 2 (Tue): Schopenhauer as Educator
S4: Different types of man that FN respects - archetypes for reference
1. Rousseau's Man (The Revolutionary / Rebel)
- Core Drive: A yearning for raw naturalness, feeling, and liberation from the artificial, corrupt constraints of society.
- Character: Rousseau's man is filled with holy wrath against social injustice, hypocrisy, and the comfortable lies of civilization.
- Role & Danger: He is the engine of popular uprisings and revolutionary movements, but his energy can turn destructive and nihilistic.
## 2. Goethe's Man (The Contemplative / Cultured Universalist)
- Core Drive: A wish to take in the whole of life and culture, to understand and absorb rather than to overturn.
- Character: Goethe's man is calm, curious, and many-sided; he looks at the world as a great spectacle and shapes what he sees into a harmonious whole.
- Role & Danger: He is the conserving, cultivating force and a corrective to Rousseau's man, but he can slide into a passive spectator who never acts.
## 3. Schopenhauer's Man (The Truthful / Heroic Sufferer)
- Core Drive: Voluntary truthfulness at any cost, even when the truth wounds the one who speaks it.
- Character: Schopenhauer's man takes suffering upon himself; he gives up his own comfort and happiness to see the world honestly.
- Role & Danger: He is the hardest and highest of the three, a model of self-overcoming, but his honesty borders on a life-denying asceticism.
FN (SE §4): "The man of Schopenhauer voluntarily takes upon himself the suffering involved in being truthful."
FN (SE §1): "Be yourself! All you are now doing, thinking, desiring, is not you yourself."
Prof: the essay is less about Schopenhauer's doctrines and more about Schopenhauer as an exemplar - a life you can copy, not a system you can learn.
Prof: education = liberation; your true educators show you that your "true self" lies above you, not hidden inside you.
FN (SE §1): "Your true nature lies not concealed deep within you but immeasurably high above you."
My critique: FN says "be yourself," then hands us three ready-made templates to copy. Isn't choosing an archetype the opposite of becoming who you are?
My take: the three men map loosely onto three temperaments - action, contemplation, truthfulness. I suspect FN thinks a higher culture needs all three, not one.
Prof: hint for later - watch how Schopenhauer's man gets transformed once FN drops Schopenhauer's pessimism.
Week 2 (Thu): Schopenhauer as Educator S5-S6, culture and the genius
Prof: the goal of culture is the production of the genius; the scholar and the state serve smaller ends.
FN (SE §6): "Mankind must work continually at the production of individual great men - this and nothing else is its task."
My critique: this is openly elitist. If the purpose of the many is to produce the few, ordinary lives become mere means. I am not convinced the "great man" justifies that cost.
Prof: compare with the scholar - the scholar is useful but small; he serves the state and the market, not culture.
Prof: FN calls the comfortable educated class "philistines of culture" - they collect culture the way Goethe's man would, but without his seriousness.
Prof: the essay's real target is "Bildung" - German education that produces scholars who know everything about culture and nothing about how to live.
FN (SE §6): "The scholar ... is nothing but a mirror; he is not an end in himself."
My take: this is where I see FN first turning against Goethe's man. The cultured universalist, cut off from action, becomes the scholar who only mirrors.
Reading for Tuesday: GS Preface + §1-34
Week 3 (Tue): The Gay Science Book I - the gay science as method
Prof: "gay science" = la gaya scienza, the Provençal art of the troubadours; knowledge pursued with lightness, laughter, and courage.
Prof: the preface (2nd ed., 1887) is written from the far side of illness - convalescence as a lens for philosophy.
FN (GS Preface §3): "We philosophers are not free to divide body from soul ... we have to give birth to our thoughts out of our pain."
FN (GS Preface §4): "Those Greeks were superficial - out of profundity."
My take: "superficial out of profundity" is the key to the whole book: you only earn lightness after you have stared into the abyss. This is Schopenhauer's man who has learned to laugh.
Prof: GS §1 - the teachers of the purpose of existence; every moralist makes life seem worth living by inventing a purpose. Laughter will outlast them.
FN (GS §1): "To laugh at oneself as one would have to laugh in order to laugh out of the whole truth."
Prof: GS §2 - the intellectual conscience: most people do not find it contemptible to believe this or that without weighing the reasons.
FN (GS §2): "The great majority of people lacks an intellectual conscience."
My critique: FN demands an intellectual conscience from everyone, but later he questions the "will to truth" itself (GS §344). Which is it? Is honesty a virtue or one more prejudice?
Prof: the answer comes in Book V - honesty turned on itself becomes the self-overcoming of morality.
Prof: method point - the aphorism form is deliberate. Short sections force the reader to ruminate, "like a cow," instead of extracting a system.
Week 3 (Thu): GS Book II - "we artists" and joyful wisdom
Prof: GS §57-59 - "to the realists": you think you see reality soberly, but your sobriety is still a passion and a fantasy.
Prof: GS §78 - we should be grateful to artists for teaching us to see ourselves as heroes from a distance, to simplify and transfigure.
FN (GS §78): "Only artists have given men eyes and ears to hear and see with some pleasure what each man is himself."
FN (GS §107): "As an aesthetic phenomenon existence is still bearable for us."
FN (GS §107): "We need all exuberant, floating, dancing, mocking, childish, and blissful art lest we lose the freedom above things."
My take: GS §107 is the sequel to The Birth of Tragedy - art is no longer metaphysical comfort but a kind of good will toward appearance.
My critique: the claim that art makes existence "bearable" quietly admits Schopenhauer's premise that existence is unbearable on its own. FN has not fully escaped pessimism here.
Prof: "joyful wisdom" is not a mood; it is a discipline of affirming knowledge without needing it to console us.
Prof: "we artists" = we who know that appearance is appearance and still love it. Contrast with Goethe's man, who loves appearance without suspecting it.
Prof: GS §113-114 - art and knowledge used to fight; the "gay science" is the attempt to make them allies.
FN (GS §110): "To what extent can truth endure incorporation? That is the question; that is the experiment."
My take: "incorporation" is the method word. A truth is not known until it is lived, the way a style is not real until it is practiced.
Reading for Tuesday: GS §108-125, and skim §109-112 on the shadows of God
Week 4 (Tue): GS §108 - shadows of God
FN (GS §108): "God is dead; but given the way of men, there may still be caves for thousands of years in which his shadow will be shown."
Prof: the shadows = the habits of thought that survived the belief: a purposive nature, laws of nature, the "true world," a moral order.
FN (GS §109): "Let us beware of thinking that the world is a living being ... let us beware of saying that there are laws in nature."
Prof: §109 is a de-deification program: remove purpose, order, beauty, and wisdom from our picture of nature.
My take: the "shadows of God" reading makes atheism much harder than it sounds - you can stop believing in God and still think like a believer about science, history, and morality.
Prof: §110-112 - knowledge grew out of useful errors (things, substances, equal cases). Truth is the youngest and weakest force in us.
Week 4 (Thu): GS §125 - the madman
Prof: setting - the madman lights a lantern in the bright morning and runs to the marketplace crying "I seek God!"
Prof: the audience is not believers; they are atheists who laugh at him. The joke is that they do not grasp what they have done.
FN (GS §125): "God is dead. God remains dead. And we have killed him."
FN (GS §125): "How shall we comfort ourselves, the murderers of all murderers?"
FN (GS §125): "Is not the greatness of this deed too great for us? Must we ourselves not become gods simply to appear worthy of it?"
FN (GS §125): "I have come too early; my time is not yet."
Prof: three questions: who killed God (we did - through truthfulness, science, Christian honesty), what follows (loss of horizon, "unchaining this earth from its sun"), and who can bear it.
My critique: the madman is a lot like Rousseau's man - a prophet of upheaval in the marketplace, full of holy wrath. But Rousseau's man wants to tear down the false order; the madman mourns that it is already gone. Same temperament, opposite mood.
My take: "must we not become gods" is the seed of the Übermensch. The death of God leaves a vacancy that a new type has to fill.
Prof: the ending - he sings a requiem aeternam deo in the churches: "What are these churches now if they are not the tombs and sepulchers of God?"
My critique: FN treats the death of God as a historical fact about Europe. But plenty of people still believe. Is it an empirical claim, or a claim about what educated people are entitled to believe?
Prof: §124 just before - "in the horizon of the infinite": we have left the land and burned the bridge behind us. Freedom and terror at once.
FN (GS §124): "We have left the land and have embarked. We have burned our bridges behind us - indeed, we have gone farther and destroyed the land behind us."
My take: §124 and §125 belong together - the open sea is the same event as the dead God, once from the side of freedom and once from the side of loss.
Exam review note: be able to explain why the marketplace atheists are the real target of §125.
Reading for Tuesday: GS §270-290 and §341
Week 5 (Tue): GS §290 - giving style to one's character
FN (GS §290): "One thing is needful - to 'give style' to one's character - a great and rare art!"
FN (GS §290): "Here a large mass of second nature has been added; there a piece of original nature has been removed - both times through long practice and daily work at it."
Prof: style = a single taste governing the whole; weaknesses are not removed but reinterpreted, made to look artistic from a distance.
Prof: GS §290 links back to §78 - we become the artists and the poets of our own lives.
FN (GS §299): "We want to be the poets of our life - first of all in the smallest, most everyday matters."
My take: giving style is the practical answer to "be yourself" from SE §1. You do not discover yourself; you compose yourself, the way Goethe composed a life.
My critique: "giving style" sounds like it rewards people with time and money. A laborer working 60 hours a week has no "daily work" left for composing a character. FN's ethics may assume leisure.
Prof: GS §270 - "What does your conscience say?"
FN (GS §270): "You shall become the person you are."
Prof: "become who you are" is not a paradox if the self is a task, not a fact - the same idea as SE §1, ten years later.
Week 5 (Thu): GS §341 - eternal recurrence, "the greatest weight"
Prof: §341 is framed as a thought experiment - a demon steals into your loneliest loneliness.
FN (GS §341): "This life as you now live it and have lived it, you will have to live once more and innumerable times more."
FN (GS §341): "Would you not throw yourself down and gnash your teeth and curse the demon who spoke thus? Or have you once experienced a tremendous moment when you would have answered him: 'You are a god and never have I heard anything more divine.'"
Prof: two readings: cosmological (FN thought time actually repeats) vs. practical (a test of how well disposed you are to your own life). The course leans toward the practical reading.
Prof: the "greatest weight" replaces the weight God used to give to actions - a new center of gravity after §125.
My take: eternal recurrence is the opposite of Schopenhauer's man. Schopenhauer's man would answer the demon with a curse - the honest pessimist says "never again." Affirming recurrence keeps Schopenhauer's truthfulness but drops his No.
My critique: as a test, recurrence seems to reward people who had lucky lives. Someone who survived a terrible trauma and does not "crave nothing more fervently" than its return is not showing weakness.
Prof: reply to that objection - affirmation is not liking each event; it is refusing to want the whole cut apart, since the suffering is woven into who you became.
My take: I find that reply better, but it is close to a theodicy - every suffering is justified because it made me me. FN would hate being compared to Leibniz.
Prof: GS §342 closes Book IV with "Incipit tragoedia" - the first section of Zarathustra, almost word for word.
Prof: recurrence is also how FN tests style: a character with style is one you could will again, every detail included.
My take: so Week 5 is one idea in two halves - §290 shapes the life, §341 asks whether you could will the shaped life forever.
Reading for Tuesday: Genealogy of Morals, Preface and First Essay
Week 6 (Tue): Genealogy of Morals, First Essay - good and bad, good and evil
Prof: the question of the Genealogy - what is the value of our values? Under what conditions did man invent the judgments good and evil?
FN (GM Preface §6): "We need a critique of moral values; the value of these values themselves must first be called into question."
Prof: master morality starts from self-affirmation: "we noble, we good, we beautiful, we happy." "Bad" is an afterthought, a pale contrast.
Prof: slave morality starts from a No to what is outside: "evil" comes first, and "good" is derived as whatever is not the evil enemy.
FN (GM I §10): "The slave revolt in morality begins when ressentiment itself becomes creative and gives birth to values."
FN (GM I §10): "While every noble morality develops from a triumphant affirmation of itself, slave morality from the outset says No to what is 'outside'."
- Master morality: good vs. bad; active; spontaneous; forgets injuries.
- Slave morality: good vs. evil; reactive; imaginary revenge; long memory for injuries.
- Example: the lambs and the birds of prey (GM I §13) - lambs call the birds evil, and call themselves good for not being birds of prey.
FN (GM I §13): "To demand of strength that it should not express itself as strength ... is just as absurd as to demand of weakness that it should express itself as strength."
My critique: the lambs-and-birds image naturalizes domination. If strength "cannot help" expressing itself, FN has no ground to blame the slave revolt either - ressentiment is also just what the weak cannot help.
My take: the sharpest point is psychological, not historical: when I call someone "evil," it is worth asking whether I am just weak and angry about it.
Prof: Rousseau's man is a borderline case - his holy wrath can be noble rebellion or plain ressentiment. FN's later writings lean toward ressentiment.
Week 6 (Thu): Genealogy, Second and Third Essays - guilt, bad conscience, the ascetic priest
Prof: Second Essay - "guilt" (Schuld) comes from "debts" (Schulden); punishment began as a creditor's compensation.
Prof: bad conscience = instincts that cannot discharge outward are turned inward.
FN (GM II §16): "All instincts that do not discharge themselves outwardly turn inward - this is what I call the internalization of man."
Prof: Third Essay - the ascetic priest gives suffering a meaning: "you are suffering because of your sin." He redirects ressentiment against the sufferer himself.
FN (GM III §28): "Man would rather will nothingness than not will."
Prof: the priest is a doctor who poisons the wound while treating it - he makes the herd bearable but sicker.
My take: the ascetic priest is a dark version of Schopenhauer's man. Both take suffering seriously and both say No to life; the difference is that the priest uses truthfulness as a weapon and Schopenhauer's man turns it on himself.
My critique: FN blames the priest for making suffering meaningful, but GS §341 is also a way of making suffering meaningful. The real disagreement seems to be about which meaning, not whether to give one.
Exam review note: know the difference between "bad" and "evil," and use ressentiment in an essay without just defining it.
Prof: the Genealogy is a "polemic"; read it as a doctor's diagnosis of Europe, not as a history exam.
- Three essays, three diagnoses: ressentiment (I), bad conscience (II), the ascetic ideal (III).
- Each one names a way that suffering became a reason to say No to life.
My critique: FN writes about Judaism and "the priestly people" in a way that has been used for antisemitic ends. Even if he despised the antisemites of his day, the rhetoric is careless at best.
Reading for Tuesday: Zarathustra, Prologue and "On the Three Metamorphoses"
Week 7 (Tue): Thus Spoke Zarathustra - the Prologue
Prof: Zarathustra comes down from the mountain after ten years of solitude, "going under" like the evening sun.
Prof: the first person he meets is the old saint in the forest who has not heard that God is dead - a callback to §125.
FN (Z Prologue §2): "Could it be possible! This old saint in the forest has not yet heard anything of this, that God is dead!"
FN (Z Prologue §3): "I teach you the Übermensch. Man is something that shall be overcome. What have you done to overcome him?"
FN (Z Prologue §4): "Man is a rope, tied between beast and Übermensch - a rope over an abyss."
Prof: the Übermensch is "the meaning of the earth" - a goal that replaces the otherworldly goal God provided.
FN (Z Prologue §3): "Remain faithful to the earth, and do not believe those who speak to you of otherworldly hopes!"
Prof: the last man is the opposite pole: comfortable, safe, equal, small. "We have invented happiness," say the last men, and they blink.
FN (Z Prologue §5): "'What is love? What is creation? What is longing? What is a star?' thus asks the last man, and he blinks."
Prof: the crowd shouts "Give us this last man, O Zarathustra!" - they want the last man, not the Übermensch.
My take: the last man is the marketplace atheist of §125 grown up and comfortable. He killed God and then moved into the house.
My critique: the Übermensch is surprisingly close to Goethe's man - many-sided, self-composed, at peace with the whole. FN later calls Goethe the last German he reveres. But Goethe's man is a spectator and the Übermensch is a creator. Is that difference real, or just rhetoric?
My take: maybe the Übermensch is what you get if you combine all three archetypes - Rousseau's energy, Goethe's breadth, Schopenhauer's honesty - and drop the resentment, the passivity, and the pessimism.
Week 7 (Thu): Zarathustra - "On the Three Metamorphoses"
FN (Z I "On the Three Metamorphoses"): "Three metamorphoses of the spirit I name for you: how the spirit becomes a camel, and the camel a lion, and the lion at last a child."
- Camel: the load-bearing spirit; kneels to take on the heaviest burdens; "Thou shalt." Reverence, duty, the hardest truths.
- Lion: wants to be master in its own desert; fights the great dragon "Thou shalt" with "I will." Creates freedom for new creation but cannot create values.
- Child: a new beginning, a game, a sacred Yes; the one who can create values.
FN (Z I "On the Three Metamorphoses"): "The child is innocence and forgetting, a new beginning, a game, a wheel rolling out of itself, a first movement, a sacred Yes."
Prof: one reading - the camel as Schopenhauer's man (taking on the hardest truths), the lion as Rousseau's man (a sacred No to the old order), the child as the one who can affirm recurrence.
My take: this is the cleanest map of the course so far. Camel = SE §4, lion = GS §125, child = GS §341.
My critique: the child stage sounds like a regression dressed up as a summit. "Innocence and forgetting" after all that labor? It risks endorsing thoughtlessness.
Prof: forgetting here means active forgetting (GM II §1) - the ability not to be poisoned by the past, not ignorance.
Prof: Zarathustra is self-overcoming in dramatic form; every part ends with Zarathustra going under again.
FN (Z II "On Self-Overcoming"): "And life itself confided this secret to me: 'Behold,' it said, 'I am that which must always overcome itself.'"
My take: self-overcoming is the thread from SE to Z: the educator above you (SE §1), the style you compose (GS §290), the rope over the abyss (Z Prologue §4).
Prof: Z III "The Convalescent" - the animals sing recurrence back to Zarathustra as a "hurdy-gurdy song," and he scolds them for making it easy.
FN (Z III "The Convalescent"): "Everything goes, everything comes back; eternally rolls the wheel of being."
My critique: if Zarathustra himself gets sick from the thought of recurrence, the "test" reading from Week 5 looks too tidy. The thought is meant to nearly destroy the one who thinks it.
Reading for Tuesday: BGE §1-23 and GS §354, §374
Week 8 (Tue): Perspectivism
FN (GM III §12): "There is only a perspective seeing, only a perspective 'knowing'; and the more affects we allow to speak about one thing, the more eyes we can use, the more complete will our 'concept' of this thing be."
Prof: perspectivism is not "anything goes." More perspectives give a more objective view; objectivity = the ability to control and switch your perspectives.
Prof: GS §374 - "our new infinite": we cannot see around our own corner, so the world may contain infinite interpretations.
FN (GS §374): "We cannot look around our own corner."
Prof: GS §354 - consciousness developed for communication, so what we are conscious of is the herd's average, not the individual.
My critique: perspectivism seems to undercut itself. If every claim is a perspective, then "every claim is a perspective" is too, and FN's critique of slave morality is just one more angle.
My take: the better reading is that perspectivism is a claim about the shape of knowledge (embodied, interested, plural), not a claim that truth does not exist. It is how FN can still say the priest is wrong.
Week 8 (Thu): Will to power and amor fati
Prof: will to power appears first as a psychological hypothesis (BGE §13), later as a general interpretation of all events.
FN (BGE §13): "A living thing seeks above all to discharge its strength - life itself is will to power; self-preservation is only one of the indirect and most frequent results."
Prof: will to power is not domination of others first; its highest form is self-overcoming and the giving of form - artists and philosophers.
My take: "will to power as giving form" connects straight back to GS §290 - giving style to one's character is will to power aimed at oneself.
My critique: FN's examples of will to power keep sliding between artistic self-shaping and political conquest. The Genealogy's "blond beast" is not someone giving style to his character.
FN (GS §276): "I want to learn more and more to see as beautiful what is necessary in things; then I shall be one of those who make things beautiful. Amor fati: let that be my love henceforth!"
FN (GS §276): "Some day I wish to be only a Yes-sayer."
FN (EH "Why I Am So Clever" §10): "My formula for greatness in a human being is amor fati: that one wants nothing to be different, not forward, not backward, not in all eternity."
Prof: amor fati is the emotional side of eternal recurrence; recurrence is the test, amor fati is passing it.
My take: amor fati answers the pessimism question from Week 3. FN does not deny suffering like a cheerful optimist; he loves the whole, suffering included. That is how he leaves Schopenhauer behind.
My critique: "wanting nothing to be different" is hard to square with FN's own demand for a revaluation of all values. If I love fate, why fight the priests?
Prof: possible reply - the fight is part of the fate. You can love a world in which you struggle.
## Exam review
Exam review: the final is two essays out of five prompts; one prompt asks you to trace a concept across at least three texts.
- Death of God: GS §108, §125; Z Prologue §2; link to the last man.
- Self-overcoming: SE §1 ("your true nature lies above you"), GS §270, §290, Z II "On Self-Overcoming", the three metamorphoses.
- Art and style: GS Preface, §78, §107, §290, §299; will to power as giving form.
- Morality: GM I master/slave and ressentiment; GM II bad conscience; GM III the ascetic priest; perspectivism as method.
- Suffering and pessimism: Schopenhauer's man, GS §107, §341, amor fati, the priest's interpretation of suffering.
- Archetypes: Rousseau's man (madman, lion), Goethe's man (Übermensch?, giving style), Schopenhauer's man (camel, ascetic priest).
Exam review: Prof said not to treat the Übermensch as a biological or political program; read it as an ideal of self-overcoming.
Exam review: know at least two readings of eternal recurrence and argue for one.
My take: for the tracing essay I want to follow Schopenhauer's man from SE §4 to GS §341 to the camel - the story of how truthfulness stops being life-denying.
My critique: one thing the course never settled - does FN have a positive ethics at all, or only a diagnosis? Giving style, amor fati, and the Übermensch are all forms, not contents.
